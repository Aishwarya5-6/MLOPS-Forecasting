\begin{abstract}
MLOps platforms often replace a production forecasting model automatically when a newly trained
challenger scores better on recent validation data. How much better it must be is usually set by
hand, and the effect of that choice is rarely measured. We build a reproducible forecasting
pipeline for hourly PM\textsubscript{2.5} with versioned data, walk-forward evaluation and a model
registry, and use it to replay 52 weekly promotion decisions on each of \R{n_datasets} datasets:
CAMS reanalysis estimates for Hyderabad and CPCB station measurements for Hyderabad, Delhi,
Bengaluru and Chennai. We compare relative-improvement thresholds of 0--10\%, a Diebold--Mariano
(DM) test rule and a never-switch rule, each with 7-, 14- and 30-day validation windows, under a
protocol fixed before any result was computed. Promoting whenever the challenger is better
(\(\tau=0\)) swapped the production model \R{range/tau0/7/swaps} times a year with a 7-day window,
and \R{range/tau0/7/harmful_pct} of those swaps made the following week worse. A 5\% threshold
with a 30-day window cut swaps to \R{range/tau5/30/swaps} a year while changing deployed RMSE by
only \R{range/tau5/30/rel_tau0_pct}. The pre-registered hypotheses that thresholds (H1) and longer
windows (H3) reduce harmful swaps held in \R{H1/holds}/\R{H1/cells} and \R{H3/holds}/\R{H3/cells}
settings; the hypothesis that a DM test beats fixed thresholds (H2) held in only
\R{H2/holds}/\R{H2/cells}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Forecasting models in production are retrained as new data arrive. A common way to decide
whether a retrained or new model should replace the current one is a \emph{champion--challenger}
rule: score both on a recent window and promote the challenger if it is better, often by a fixed
margin. Tools such as MLflow~\cite{zaharia2018} make this a few lines of code, using registry
aliases that downstream services resolve at load time. Surveys of MLOps practice describe automated
retraining and promotion as a core component~\cite{kreuzberger2023}, and production experience shows
that the surrounding ``glue'' decisions carry hidden costs~\cite{sculley2015,breck2017}.

The promotion margin, however, is usually a guess. If it is too small, the system follows noise: the
challenger is, by construction, the best of several candidates on the validation window, so its score is
optimistically biased~\cite{cawley2010}, and a swap may make the next week \emph{worse}. If it is
too large, real improvements are kept out of production. Statistical forecast-comparison tests such
as Diebold--Mariano (DM)~\cite{diebold1995,harvey1997} offer an alternative, but they need enough
validation data to have power.

We ask: \emph{how do the promotion rule and the validation-window length affect harmful swaps
and the accuracy of the model actually deployed?} Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item An open, reproducible forecasting platform (DVC, MLflow, walk-forward evaluation, leakage
        tests, registry automation) for hourly PM\textsubscript{2.5}, used as the testbed.
  \item A \emph{deployment replay} that turns one year of data into 52 promotion decisions, each
        judged on the following, unseen week.
  \item A pre-registered comparison of seven rules and three validation windows on reanalysis
        data and station data from four cities, with block-bootstrap intervals and DM tests on
        deployed accuracy.
\end{enumerate}
The short answer: most automatic swaps are noise, a modest threshold on a month of validation
data removes most of them at no measurable cost, and a significance test is too cautious at
weekly decision frequency.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Forecast evaluation.} Out-of-sample evaluation with rolling origins is the standard for
time series~\cite{tashman2000}; cross-validation can be valid for autoregressive models under
conditions~\cite{bergmeir2018}. The DM test~\cite{diebold1995} compares the accuracy of two
\emph{forecasts}, with a small-sample correction by Harvey et al.~\cite{harvey1997}; Diebold later
warned against using it to compare \emph{models}~\cite{diebold2015}. We use it in the way he recommends: on
the forecasts a policy actually served. The model confidence set~\cite{hansen2011} generalises
pairwise tests to many models. Combining forecasts often beats choosing one~\cite{timmermann2006},
which motivates our ensemble candidate.

\textbf{Selection bias.} Choosing the best of several models on one validation set overfits that
set~\cite{cawley2010}. A champion--challenger rule repeats this selection every week, so the bias
compounds into unnecessary swaps.

\textbf{MLOps and retraining.} MLOps architectures~\cite{kreuzberger2023} and tooling~\cite{zaharia2018}
treat promotion as an automated step. Work on concept drift~\cite{gama2014} studies \emph{when the
data change}; recent work frames \emph{when to retrain} as a cost trade-off~\cite{regol2025}.
We study the complementary question of \emph{when to replace} the served model, given candidates.

\section{Testbed: A Reproducible Forecasting Pipeline}

\textbf{Data.} Hourly PM\textsubscript{2.5} from two sources. \emph{CAMS}: Copernicus
atmospheric-composition model estimates~\cite{inness2019} for Hyderabad via the Open-Meteo API,
Aug 2022--Nov 2026 (\R{cams/hours} hours, no gaps). \emph{CPCB}: city-level station measurements,
2015--2020, from a public compilation of Central Pollution Control Board data~\cite{rao2020kaggle},
for Hyderabad (\R{station/hours} hours, \R{station/missing_pct} missing after interpolating gaps of
up to 6\,h), Delhi (\R{station_delhi/missing_pct} missing), Bengaluru
(\R{station_bengaluru/missing_pct}) and Chennai (\R{station_chennai/missing_pct}). Kolkata and Mumbai
were excluded by a pre-registered eligibility rule (at least two years of history before the
replay); no other city was substituted. Raw files and every derived artifact are versioned with
DVC, and one command (\texttt{dvc repro}) rebuilds every number in this paper.

\textbf{Task and evaluation.} 24-hour-ahead forecasts issued daily at midnight (local time).
Models implement \texttt{fit(train)} once and \texttt{predict(history, h)} from any later origin
without refitting; a test asserts that \texttt{predict} never sees data at or after the origin.
Hyperparameters were chosen with five expanding-window folds spread over one year, and XGBoost was
tuned with 40 Optuna~\cite{akiba2019} trials.

\textbf{Candidates.} Seasonal naive (same hour yesterday); SARIMA(1,0,1)(1,0,1)\textsubscript{24}
on log values; two direct multi-horizon XGBoost models (hand-set and tuned); a two-layer LSTM;
and an equal-weight ensemble of tuned XGBoost and SARIMA. To limit compute, the three additional
cities use the five candidates without the LSTM; Hyderabad CPCB is also replayed without the LSTM
as a like-for-like control (reported in the repository, not counted in the hypothesis totals).
Hyperparameters were tuned on CAMS only and reused unchanged.

\textbf{Registry.} Each trained model is logged to MLflow with its code version, data hash and
metrics. A promotion script registers a run, gives it the \texttt{@challenger} alias, scores it
and the \texttt{@champion} on a holdout window, and moves \texttt{@champion} only if the rule
passes. Decisions and reasons are stored as version tags, and a one-command rollback follows the
recorded previous champion.

\subsection{Testbed Details}

\textbf{Evaluation design.} Hyperparameters were selected on five expanding-window folds whose
30-day test windows start 73 days apart, so together they cover a full year of seasons; the last
30 days of the CAMS series form a holdout used only by the promotion script. Within a test window
a forecast is issued every day at midnight for the next 24 hours, and each model sees only the
history strictly before the origin. Two automated tests enforce this: a spy model records every
series passed to \texttt{fit} and \texttt{predict} and fails if any timestamp reaches the origin,
and a feature test checks that every lag and rolling feature uses past values only.

\textbf{Models and tuning.} Table~\ref{tab:cv} lists the cross-validation scores of the six
candidates. XGBoost is a direct multi-horizon model (one model, forecast step as a feature) on
log values. It was tuned with \R{sweep/trials} Optuna TPE trials (best CV RMSE \R{sweep/best});
fANOVA importance attributed \R{sweep/imp/learning_rate} of the variance to the learning rate,
\R{sweep/imp/n_estimators} to the number of trees and \R{sweep/imp/min_child_weight} to the
minimum child weight; depth and the two subsampling rates each received about
\R{sweep/imp/max_depth}. The LSTM
reads seven days of scaled log values plus hour and weekday encodings and outputs all 24 steps.

\section{Promotion Policies and Replay}

\textbf{Replay.} For each dataset, the last 365 days form the replay period. Every candidate is
refit every 30 days on all data before the refit time, and its forecasts for every midnight origin
are cached (\(\approx\)395 origins \(\times\) 24 steps). At decision points \(t_k = t_0 + 7k\) days,
\(k=0,\dots,51\):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item each candidate's RMSE is computed on the validation window, the last \(V\) origins before \(t_k\);
  \item the challenger is the best non-champion candidate on that window;
  \item the policy decides whether to swap;
  \item the champion serves the next 7 origins, which are scored.
\end{enumerate}
All policies start from the same initial champion: the best candidate on the 30 days before \(t_0\).

\textbf{Policies.} \emph{Threshold-\(\tau\)}: promote if
\((\mathrm{RMSE}_\text{champ}-\mathrm{RMSE}_\text{chall})/\mathrm{RMSE}_\text{champ}\ge\tau\),
\(\tau\in\{0,1,2,5,10\}\%\). This is the same function the production promotion script calls.
\emph{DM}: compute the per-origin mean squared error, take the daily loss differential, and promote
if a one-sided HLN-corrected DM test~\cite{harvey1997} gives \(p<0.05\) in the challenger's favour.
Daily forecasts do not overlap, so \(h=1\). \emph{Never}: keep the initial champion.
\emph{Oracle}: serve each week's best model in hindsight; this is a bound, not a policy.

\textbf{Metrics.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Deployed RMSE}: the RMSE over all 52 served weeks.
  \item \emph{Swaps}: how often the champion changes.
  \item \emph{Harmful swaps}: swaps after which the new champion's RMSE over the next week is higher
        than the old champion's would have been.
  \item \emph{Excess over the oracle}: how much higher the deployed RMSE is than the oracle's.
\end{itemize}
Uncertainty comes from a week-block bootstrap (2,000 resamples, paired across policies), and
significance from two-sided DM tests on daily deployed losses.

\textbf{Pre-registration.} The policies, metrics and hypotheses below were committed to the
repository (commit \texttt{\R{protocol_commit}}) before any replay was run. The addition of the
three cities, the eligibility rule and the reduced candidate set was committed as a recorded
deviation (D1), also before any result. After the first results we made only analysis-level
changes, recorded as deviation D2 (\texttt{\R{deviation_commit}}): we added the DM-versus-\(\tau=2\%\)
bootstrap interval that H2's criterion requires but the first analysis script did not compute;
we report the no-LSTM control separately rather than as an extra dataset (counting it would give
H1 \R{H1/holds_all}/\R{H1/cells_all}, H2 \R{H2/holds_all}/\R{H2/cells_all}, H3
\R{H3/holds_all}/\R{H3/cells_all}); and we replaced one figure with paired differences. No policy,
metric, criterion, dataset or candidate changed. Each hypothesis is scored mechanically in every
(dataset, \(V\)) cell:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1}: \(\tau=0\) makes more harmful swaps than both \(\tau=2\%\) and \(\tau=5\%\),
        and \(\tau=2\%\) is not significantly less accurate than \(\tau=0\).
  \item \textbf{H2}: the DM rule has a lower harmful-swap rate than \(\tau=2\%\), and the bootstrap
        interval of their RMSE difference includes zero or favours DM.
  \item \textbf{H3}: for \(\tau\in\{0,1,2\}\%\), harmful swaps do not increase from \(V=7\) to 14 to 30 days.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Zero-threshold promotion mostly follows noise (H1: \R{H1/holds}/\R{H1/cells} cells).}
With \(\tau=0\) and a 7-day window, the production model changed \R{range/tau0/7/swaps} times in 52
weeks, and \R{range/tau0/7/harmful_pct} of these swaps were harmful, close to a coin flip
(Fig.~\ref{fig:swaps}). Even with a 30-day window, \(\tau=0\) swapped \R{range/tau0/30/swaps} times.
Raising the threshold to 2\% or 5\% removed most of these swaps
(\R{range/tau5/30/swaps} per year at \(\tau=5\%\), \(V=30\)) while deployed RMSE changed by only
\R{range/tau2/30/rel_tau0_pct} (\(\tau=2\%\)) and \R{range/tau5/30/rel_tau0_pct} (\(\tau=5\%\))
relative to \(\tau=0\) (Fig.~\ref{fig:cities}). The best single cell was \(\tau=5\%\), \(V=30\) on
CAMS, \R{cams/tau5/30/rel_tau0_pct} versus \(\tau=0\) (95\% CI \R{cams/tau5/30/rel_tau0_ci}) and
significantly better than never switching (DM \(p=\R{cams/tau5/30/dm_p_vs_never}\)).
The largest harmful swap of this kind: on \R{ex/date}, with \(\tau=0\) and \(V=7\) on CAMS, the \R{ex/from} was
replaced by the \R{ex/to} because the latter had scored better over the previous week; over the
following week the \R{ex/to} scored RMSE \R{ex/new} against the \R{ex/from}'s \R{ex/old}
(\R{ex/worse_pct} worse).

\textbf{Longer validation windows help (H3: \R{H3/holds}/\R{H3/cells}).} For thresholds up to 2\%,
harmful swaps did not increase as the window grew from 7 to 30 days in most settings. A week of
validation data (168 hourly errors per model, strongly autocorrelated) is too little to separate
candidates whose true accuracy differs by a few percent.

\textbf{The DM rule is too cautious (H2: \R{H2/holds}/\R{H2/cells}).} With one loss differential
per day, the test has only \(V\) observations and rarely reaches \(p<0.05\): it made
\R{range/dm/7/swaps} swaps a year at \(V=7\) and \R{range/dm/30/swaps} at \(V=30\). When it did
swap, the swap was often harmful (\R{range/dm/30/harmful_pct} at \(V=30\)), although with so
few swaps these rates are very noisy. It met H2 in all windows only on Bengaluru, and in two of
three on Chennai. Table~\ref{tab:hyp} lists every cell.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\textbf{Practical guidance.} For a forecasting service that re-evaluates weekly, the results support
a simple default: compare challenger and champion on about a month of recent forecasts and promote
only for a relative improvement of roughly 2--5\%. This keeps the production model stable
(fewer deployments, fewer rollbacks, easier monitoring) at no measurable cost in accuracy. A
significance test is a principled alternative, but at weekly frequency it has too little data to
act, and it is not more reliable when it does. Thresholds of 10\% or never switching are only safe
if the initial model is known to be good.

\textbf{Why switching gains little.} The challenger is the best of several candidates on the
validation window, so its advantage is inflated by selection~\cite{cawley2010}, while true
differences between reasonable models are small. This mirrors the forecast-combination
literature, where a fixed combination is hard to beat~\cite{timmermann2006}: here, the equal-weight
ensemble was the best single candidate in \R{ensemble_best_static} of \R{n_datasets} datasets.

\textbf{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Four cities in one country and five or six candidates. The size of the candidate pool sets
        how much the challenger's score is inflated, so larger pools would likely need stricter rules.
  \item The CAMS series are model estimates. The CPCB series are measurements but end in 2020, and
        their replay year includes the COVID-19 lockdown, kept as a stress test.
  \item Hyperparameters were tuned on CAMS only; the three additional cities exclude the LSTM.
  \item 52 decisions per setting give few swaps, so harmful-swap counts are descriptive, and the
        DM tests on deployed accuracy have limited power.
  \item Swaps are free in our replay. Real deployments add costs (validation, rollout, downstream
        changes), which would favour stricter rules further.
\end{itemize}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}

Automated champion--challenger promotion is a one-line rule in modern MLOps tooling, but its
threshold matters. In a pre-registered replay over five PM\textsubscript{2.5} datasets, promoting
on any improvement swapped the production model \R{range/tau0/7/swaps} times a year with a
one-week window, and \R{range/tau0/7/harmful_pct} of those swaps made the next week worse. A 2--5\% threshold on a 30-day window
removed most swaps without hurting accuracy, while a significance-test rule was too cautious to
help. Future work: larger candidate pools, other forecasting domains, and promotion rules that
account for the size of the candidate pool explicitly.

\section{Protocol and Deviations}
\label{app:protocol}
The full protocol is \texttt{docs/study-protocol.md} in the repository; its git history
(\R{protocol_history}, oldest first) records the original pre-registration, deviation D1 and
deviation D2.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Fixed before any result:} research question; datasets and cleaning; replay design
        (last 365 days, 30-day refits, 52 weekly decisions, 7-day deployment weeks); the
        candidates; the initial-champion rule; all policies and windows; the metrics, bootstrap and
        DM tests; hypotheses H1--H3 and their scoring criteria; the primary configuration
        (\(\tau=2\%\), \(V=30\)).
  \item \textbf{D1, before any result:} three additional cities, a mechanical eligibility rule
        (\(\ge\)80\% of hours present in the replay period and \(\ge\)730 days of earlier history,
        which excluded Kolkata and Mumbai), five candidates without the LSTM for those cities, a
        no-LSTM control for Hyderabad CPCB, and a 4-thread limit for the station LSTM runs.
  \item \textbf{D2, after the first results:} the DM-versus-\(\tau=2\%\) interval needed by H2;
        reporting the control separately; paired-difference and count figures; labelling of the
        exploratory analyses. No criterion changed.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproducibility Details}
\label{app:repro}
All code, configuration and the DVC pipeline are public. \texttt{dvc pull} retrieves the raw data
and forecast caches; \texttt{dvc repro} rebuilds the prepared series, caches (if inputs changed),
replays, hypothesis scores, figures, tables and \texttt{numbers.tex}, from which every number in
this paper is read. A test suite covers leakage (spy model, feature lags, cache refits), the
metrics, the DM statistic against a hand calculation, the bootstrap, the promotion rule and the
replay on synthetic data with known answers. Software: Python 3.11, MLflow \R{ver/mlflow},
DVC \R{ver/dvc}, XGBoost \R{ver/xgboost}, statsmodels \R{ver/statsmodels}, PyTorch
\R{ver/torch}, Optuna \R{ver/optuna}, pandas \R{ver/pandas}. All experiments ran on a single
laptop CPU (Apple M4); the forecast caches took about 1.5 hours in total, almost all of it LSTM
refits, and the replays take seconds.
